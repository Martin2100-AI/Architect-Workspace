UX Designer Field Guide

The 20% of user-experience design an AI Solution Architect needs to direct, evaluate, and approve AI-generated UX work — taught against one real, concrete initiative from start to finish.

The worked example

Industry: Healthcare — regional multi-clinic delivery network.

Organization: Meridian Health Network (fictional) — 14 primary-care clinics and 60+ affiliated specialists across a mid-size metro region.

Initiative: CareLink, a patient-facing referral and intake experience that lets a patient see the status of a specialist referral, schedule the appointment, upload insurance documents, and message a care coordinator — instead of waiting on a fax and a phone tag loop.

Why it matters: Meridian loses roughly 32% of specialist referrals before the patient ever books an appointment ("referral leakage"), and the average time from referral issuance to a scheduled visit is 11.4 days. Every deliverable below is built against this problem.

Architect's framing

You will rarely draw a wireframe yourself. You will review one an AI agent produced, decide whether it reflects a real user's job-to-be-done, check it against accessibility and heuristic standards, and approve or send it back with specific, cited reasons. This guide is built to make that review fast and defensible — every document below models exactly the artifact you'll be judging in production work.

Learn — The Discipline

Why UX Exists

UX is not decoration. It is the discipline of reducing the distance between what a system can do and what a person can actually accomplish with it — and for an AI Solution Architect, it's the layer that determines whether your architecture ships or gets abandoned at the login screen.

The architecture is not the product

A technically correct system with a confusing referral-status screen still produces the outcome Meridian is trying to eliminate: a patient who gives up. Your data model, your APIs, and your AI orchestration layer are necessary but not sufficient — the interface is where the business outcome is actually won or lost.

UX is a risk-reduction discipline

Every unclear label, missing error state, or low-contrast button is a defect with the same shape as a software bug: it produces a wrong or abandoned outcome under a predictable condition. Treat it with the same rigor — reproduce it, cite the standard it violates, and verify the fix.

Where AI changes the job

Generative tools can now draft a persona, a wireframe, or a full page of React in seconds. That collapses the production bottleneck but does nothing for the judgment bottleneck. An AI Solution Architect's UX value has shifted almost entirely from "can you produce this" to "can you tell whether what was produced is correct, accessible, and aligned to a real user's job." That judgment is the ~20% this guide teaches.

The Core 20%

Nine concepts cover the large majority of what you need to direct and evaluate AI-generated UX work. Each links to the CareLink deliverable that puts it into practice.

Personas & Jobs-to-be-Done

A persona grounded in demographics ("Dolores, 68, retired") is decoration. A persona grounded in a job-to-be-done — "When my doctor refers me to a specialist, I want to know it's actually been scheduled, so I can stop worrying I fell through a crack" — is a design constraint. JTBD statements follow the pattern When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]. Evaluate AI-generated personas by asking: does every trait justify a design decision, or is it filler? See Document 1.

User Journeys

A journey map plots a persona's actions, thoughts, and emotions across every stage of a task — including the stages that happen outside your interface (a fax machine, a phone call, an insurance denial letter). The single most common failure in AI-generated journey maps is omitting the unhappy path. A journey with no failure branch is a marketing diagram, not a UX artifact. See Document 2.

Information Architecture

IA is the structure and labeling of content and navigation — organized around the words and mental model of the user, not the org chart or the database schema. The target for core tasks in almost any consumer or patient-facing system is no more than three clicks from the home screen. See Document 3.

Wireframes, Mockups, Prototypes

Three distinct fidelity levels, each with a different job:

ArtifactShowsDoes not show
WireframeLayout, hierarchy, content structure, statesColor, type, brand, final copy
MockupFull visual design at pixel fidelityInteraction, motion, response to input
PrototypeClickable/simulated behavior for testingProduction-grade code, real data

Approving a mockup for visual polish when the underlying wireframe never resolved its error states is the most common AI-generated-UX review mistake. See Document 4.

Visual & Interaction Hierarchy

Size, weight, color, spacing, and position tell the eye where to go first. In CareLink, the primary action on the referral card ("Schedule appointment") must out-rank secondary actions ("View details", "Message coordinator") through contrast and position — not just be present somewhere on the screen.

Design Systems & Tokens

A token is a named, semantic value — color-action-primary, not #FB2832 — so intent survives a rebrand or a dark-mode variant. Review AI-generated UI for raw hex values or pixel literals in place of tokens; that's technical debt disguised as a finished screen. See Document 7.

Usability Testing

Structured observation of real users attempting real tasks, measured against defined success criteria — not a design-team opinion poll. A five-to-eight participant moderated test surfaces the majority of usability problems (Nielsen's research puts ~85% detection at five users for a single round). See Document 8.

Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics

Jakob Nielsen's heuristics are broad rules of thumb for evaluating an interface's usability without running a full test. Use them as your default review checklist for any AI-generated screen.

#HeuristicWhat to check
1Visibility of system statusDoes the user always know what's happening — loading, saved, submitted, error?
2Match between system and the real worldDoes language match the user's vocabulary, not internal jargon (e.g. "Referral" not "Case Object")?
3User control and freedomCan the user undo, cancel, or back out of a flow without penalty?
4Consistency and standardsDo identical actions look and behave identically across every screen?
5Error preventionDoes the design stop a mistake before it happens (confirmations, constraints, defaults)?
6Recognition rather than recallIs required information visible on screen, instead of relying on memory?
7Flexibility and efficiency of useAre there accelerators for repeat/expert users without punishing novices?
8Aesthetic and minimalist designIs every element on screen earning its place, or is it noise competing with the task?
9Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errorsAre error messages in plain language, with a specific fix, not a code?
10Help and documentationIs help available in context, when needed, without derailing the task?
Severity scale used throughout this guide

0 Not a problem   1 Cosmetic — fix if time allows   2 Minor — low priority   3 Major — high priority, fix before launch   4 Catastrophe — blocks launch

Accessibility to WCAG 2.1 AA

WCAG 2.1 organizes accessibility around four principles — POUR. AA is the conformance level nearly every enterprise, healthcare, and public-sector contract requires; it is the level Meridian's own compliance office mandates for CareLink.

Perceivable

Information and UI components must be presentable in ways users can perceive — text alternatives for images, captions, sufficient color contrast, resizable text.

Operable

UI components and navigation must be operable — full keyboard access, enough time to read and act, no content that triggers seizures, clear navigation.

Understandable

Information and UI operation must be understandable — readable text, predictable behavior, input assistance and error identification.

Robust

Content must be robust enough to be interpreted reliably by assistive technology, now and as that technology evolves.

Numbers an architect should have memorized

4.5:1 minimum contrast for normal text (SC 1.4.3) · 3:1 for large text (18pt+/14pt bold+) and UI components (SC 1.4.11) · 44×44px minimum touch target (SC 2.5.5, AAA — CareLink targets it anyway for its older patient population) · 24×24px minimum target (SC 2.5.8, AA) · full functionality must be reachable by keyboard alone (SC 2.1.1) with a visible focus indicator (SC 2.4.7).

The full evidence-based checklist, with measured contrast ratios against CareLink's actual token palette, is Document 6.

Good vs Bad Design — CareLink Example

The same referral-status card, done two ways.

Bad: status card as originally proposed by a generative first draft

  • Status shown only as an internal code: REF_STAT_04
  • Primary action ("Schedule") and destructive action ("Cancel referral") styled identically
  • No indication of when the referral was issued or by whom
  • Error state on failed load renders a blank white card with no message
  • Contrast of the status label measured at 2.1:1 against its background

Good: revised after heuristic + WCAG review

  • Status shown in plain language with a color + icon + text combination: "Awaiting your scheduling — action needed"
  • Primary action styled in Cherry Red per token system; destructive action requires a confirmation step
  • Referring physician, clinic, and issue date shown directly on the card (recognition over recall)
  • Error state shows a specific message and a retry action: "We couldn't load this referral. Try again, or call your clinic at (555) 019-2200."
  • Status label contrast measured at 7.8:1

This is exactly the diff you should be able to produce in a review comment on any AI-generated screen — specific, cited, and testable.

The Architect's Review Lens

A repeatable checklist for directing, evaluating, and approving AI-generated UX artifacts — the actual job.

Before you ask the AI to generate anything

  • Is there a named persona and a job-to-be-done this screen serves?
  • Is the unhappy path (error, empty, denial, timeout) explicitly in scope for the prompt?
  • Is the accessibility conformance target (WCAG 2.1 AA) stated as a requirement, not an afterthought?

When you review what came back

  • Run it against Nielsen's 10 — cite the number, not a vibe.
  • Check every interactive element for a visible focus state and a keyboard path.
  • Check every status/color used to convey meaning has a text or icon backup (never color alone).
  • Check that colors/spacing/type trace back to named tokens, not raw values.
  • Check every list, card, or table has an explicit empty, loading, and error state — not just success.
Approval is a decision, not a formality

Approving a screen means you are willing to put your name on the claim that it serves the persona's job-to-be-done, passes the applicable heuristics, and meets WCAG 2.1 AA. Treat the sign-off block on each CareLink document below as a real commitment, not a template field.

Purpose & method

These personas were synthesized from Meridian's referral-tracking data (14 clinics, trailing 12 months), six shadowed care-coordinator shifts, and eight structured patient interviews conducted after a specialist referral. Each persona pairs a primary job-to-be-done (JTBD) with the frustrations that currently block it. Two are patients; one is the internal care coordinator whose workflow CareLink also changes — approving a patient-facing screen without accounting for the coordinator's queue is a common AI-generated-UX gap.

Dolores Fenwick

PatientAge 68Retired teacherLow visionLow digital confidence

"I don't want to call and bother anyone, but I also don't want to just sit here not knowing if I'm even on the calendar."
Job-to-be-done

When my doctor refers me to a specialist, I want to know — without making a phone call — that the referral is real and moving, so I can stop worrying I've fallen through a crack.

Goals
  • Confirm the referral was received by the specialist's office
  • Understand what she needs to do next, in plain language
  • Avoid re-explaining her situation to a new person each call
Frustrations today
  • Referral status is invisible until the specialist's office calls — often 1–2 weeks later
  • Portal text is small and low-contrast; she uses a phone magnifier

Uses an 11-year-old Android tablet over home Wi-Fi. Needs 200% zoom without layout breakage, high contrast text, and no reliance on hover-only interactions.

PatientAge 41Warehouse ops managerMobile-onlyTime-poor

"I've got maybe four minutes between shift huddles. If it takes longer than that, I'll do it later — and later never comes."
Job-to-be-done

When I get a referral during a rushed appointment, I want to book the specialist visit from my phone in under two minutes, so I don't have to remember to call during work hours I don't have.

Goals

  • See available times that fit an irregular shift schedule

Frustrations today

Android phone, cracked screen, frequently on a spotty cellular connection. Needs the flow to be resilient to slow/dropped connections and fully usable one-handed.

Priya Anand

Internal userCare CoordinatorHandles ~40 referrals/dayPower user

"I have forty of these open at once. If I can't tell what's stuck versus what's just waiting on the patient, I'm chasing ghosts all day."
Job-to-be-done

When I triage my referral queue each morning, I want to instantly separate "waiting on patient action" from "waiting on us," so I spend my limited outreach time on referrals that actually need a human.

Goals

  • Message a patient directly inside the same tool she's already in

Frustrations today

How to evaluate an AI-generated persona

Reject a persona if you can delete a trait without changing a single design decision downstream. "Dolores enjoys gardening" tells you nothing actionable; "Dolores needs 200% zoom without layout breakage" directly drives a WCAG requirement in Document 6 and a wireframe decision in Document 4.

Unhappy path — where 32% of referrals leak today

Branch point: Stage 2, Notification. If the referral notification fails to send (a known gap in the current fax-based workflow — roughly 1 in 5 referrals) or the patient's contact information is stale, Dolores never learns the referral exists. No follow-up is triggered internally because the system has no idea the loop is broken. Twelve days later, the specialist's office marks the referral "no-show scheduling" and closes it. Dolores believes she is still waiting to hear back; the coordinator believes the ball is in Dolores's court. Both are wrong, and nobody is notified of the mismatch. CareLink's fix: a coordinator-facing dashboard (Persona 3, Priya Anand) that flags any referral with no patient portal activity within 72 hours — closing the visibility gap on both sides at once.

Secondary unhappy path — insurance denial

Branch point: Stage 6, Insurance & prep. Prior-authorization is denied or delayed. Today this surfaces only as a same-day cancellation call from the specialist's front desk — after the patient has already arranged transportation and taken time off work. CareLink's fix: surface authorization status as soon as it changes, with a specific reason and a coordinator contact, at least 48 hours before the appointment.

Screen A — My Referrals (list)

Screen A — annotated success state
  1. Page title — plain-language "My Referrals," matching the sitemap label in Document 3.
  2. Filter tabs — All / Needs action / Scheduled, with a live count; "Needs action" isolates exactly what Persona 3 (Priya) and Persona 1 (Dolores) each need fastest.
  3. Referring detail — physician name and clinic shown directly on the card (recognition over recall, Heuristic 6).
  4. Status badge — plain-language status, never color alone (Heuristic 1 + WCAG 1.4.1); amber reserved for action-needed per the token sheet.
  5. Primary action — "Schedule" in Cherry Red, the only Cherry Red element on the row, so the eye lands on it first.
  6. Secondary action — "View details" as a text link, visually subordinate to the primary CTA.

Screen B — Referral Detail

State: EmptyNo documents yet
State: LoadingFetching referral
State: ErrorReferral failed to load
State: SuccessFull referral
Screen B — annotated success state
  1. Referral header — status badge repeats the plain-language pattern from Screen A for consistency (Heuristic 4).
  2. Primary CTA — "Schedule appointment," the single highest-contrast element on the screen.
  3. Destructive/secondary actions — "Message coordinator" is a secondary but styled button; "Cancel referral" is deliberately de-emphasized to a text link and requires a confirmation dialog before it fires (error prevention, Heuristic 5).
  4. Insurance status panel — closes the unhappy-path gap identified in the Journey Map by surfacing authorization status before the visit, with an expected timeframe.
  5. Documents list — shows what's already uploaded; "Add document" stays visible even when the list is non-empty.
  6. Activity timeline — read-only audit trail so the patient never has to wonder "did anything happen since I last checked."

Objective

Determine whether Dolores Fenwick and Marcus Whitfield's persona representatives can complete the five core CareLink tasks unassisted, at or above the KPI targets defined in Learn 06, before Meridian commits to the three-clinic pilot.

Methodology

FormatModerated, remote (video call + screen share), one participant per 45-minute session
PrototypeMid-fidelity clickable prototype of My Referrals + Referral Detail, built from Document 4's wireframes
Participants7 total: 3 patients aged 60+ (matching Dolores's profile, incl. 1 with low vision using screen magnification), 2 patients aged 30–45 on mobile-only connections (matching Marcus's profile), 2 internal care coordinators (matching Priya's profile)
RecruitmentPatient participants recruited via Meridian's patient advisory panel; no current CareLink exposure; compensated $75 gift card
MethodThink-aloud protocol; moderator does not assist unless the participant is fully stuck for 90+ seconds ("lostness" threshold)

Tasks, success metrics & time budgets

#TaskSuccess metricTime budget
1Find out whether your cardiology referral has been receivedLocates correct status without assistance≤ 30 sec

Moderator script (verbatim)

Introduction

"Thanks for making time today. We're testing a new tool called CareLink, not you — there's no wrong way to use it, and if something is confusing, that's exactly what we need to find. Please think out loud as you go: tell me what you're looking at, what you expect to happen, and what actually happens. I'll ask you to attempt a few realistic tasks. I won't be able to help unless you're fully stuck, so if it feels slow, that's useful information too. Any questions before we start?"

Consent & recording

Verbal consent for recording (audio, screen, and think-aloud narration) is obtained and logged before the session begins; participants are reminded they may stop at any time with no effect on compensation.

Task prompts (read verbatim, one at a time)
  • Task 1: "Your doctor mentioned she referred you to a cardiologist last week. Using CareLink, find out whether that's actually happened."
  • Task 2: "Go ahead and book that appointment for a time that works for you."
  • Task 3: "The specialist's office needs a copy of your insurance card. Add it to your referral."
  • Task 4: "You have a question about whether you need to fast before the appointment. Ask your care coordinator."
  • Task 5: "You've decided you don't want to go through with this referral after all. Cancel it, and tell me what you think will happen next."
Post-task questions (asked after each task)
  • "On a scale of 1 (very difficult) to 5 (very easy), how difficult or easy was that?"
  • "What, if anything, made you hesitate?"
  • "Was there a point where you weren't sure what would happen if you tapped something? Which one?"
Post-test questionnaire — System Usability Scale (SUS)

Standard 10-item instrument, 5-point agreement scale, administered immediately after Task 5:

  1. I think that I would like to use CareLink frequently.
  2. I found CareLink unnecessarily complex.
  3. I thought CareLink was easy to use.
  4. I think that I would need support to be able to use CareLink.
  5. I found the various functions in CareLink were well integrated.
  6. I thought there was too much inconsistency in CareLink.
  7. I would imagine that most people would learn to use CareLink very quickly.
  8. I found CareLink very cumbersome to use.
  9. I felt very confident using CareLink.
  10. I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with CareLink.
Debrief

"That's everything — thank you. Overall, was there anything that surprised you, good or bad? Is there anything you expected to be able to do in CareLink that you couldn't find?"

Success criteria for the pilot go/no-go decision

Thresholds set in advance, per Learn 06

Task success rate ≥ 80% across all 5 tasks · Median SUS ≥ 72 · No Severity 4 (catastrophe) heuristic finding surfaces during testing that wasn't already known from Document 5 · At least 2 of the 3 age-60+ participants complete Task 2 (scheduling) unassisted, since this task carries the highest referral-leakage stakes.

The problem

Meridian Health Network loses roughly 32% of specialist referrals before a patient ever books the appointment, and the average time from referral to a scheduled visit is 11.4 days — driven by a fax-and-phone workflow with no shared visibility between patient and care coordinator. Document 2's journey map traces exactly where that trust breaks down.

The initiative

CareLink gives patients direct visibility into referral status, self-service scheduling, document upload, and coordinator messaging, while giving care coordinators (Persona 3) a triage view that separates "waiting on us" from "waiting on the patient" — closing the exact visibility gap identified as the root cause of leakage.

Key findings from this evaluation

Heuristic evaluation (Document 5)

16 findings against Nielsen's 10 heuristics on the two highest-traffic screens. All Severity 3–4 findings resolved in the v1.0 wireframes; 2 minor items remain open and are tracked, not blocking.

Accessibility (Document 6)

20 of 22 applicable WCAG 2.1 AA criteria pass with measured evidence; 2 are partial (zoom edge case, one tooltip), both scheduled for remediation by 2026-09-10, ahead of pilot launch.

Projected task success rate by core task

The one number driving this recommendation

Task 2 — scheduling the appointment — projects at 76%, below the 80% go/no-go threshold set in Document 8. Every other task clears the bar. Scheduling is also the single highest-leverage task against the 32% leakage number, which makes this gap the whole ballgame rather than a rounding error.

Recommendation

Proceed to the three-clinic pilot conditional on one fix: simplify the scheduling calendar's default view (currently defaults three months out with no "soonest available" sort — see Document 2, Stage 5) before the moderated usability test in Document 8 is run. Do not delay the full pilot decision for the two open Severity-2 heuristic items or the two partial WCAG criteria; remediate those in parallel per their existing due dates.

Decision needed

For Sam Okafor, VP Product

Approve a one-week design-and-build cycle for the scheduling default-sort fix before the usability test proceeds, holding the pilot's Q1 2027 target date. Alternative: run the usability test as-is and accept the risk that Task 2 underperforms its threshold, informing the go/no-go decision directly from real user data instead of a pre-emptive fix.
